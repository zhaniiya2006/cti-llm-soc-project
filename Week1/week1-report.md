# 3. Cyber Threat Intelligence Glossary

## 3.1 Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI)

Cyber Threat Intelligence is information about cyber threats that is collected, processed and analysed to help organisations understand threats and make informed security decisions.

CTI can contain information about threat actors, attack techniques, indicators of compromise, vulnerabilities and campaigns.

For our project, CTI is the main type of information that will later be collected and processed before being analysed with the help of an LLM.

---

## 3.2 Threat Actor

A threat actor is an individual, group or organisation that conducts malicious or potentially harmful cyber activities.

Examples of threat actors can include:

- cybercriminal groups;
- hacktivist groups;
- insider threats;
- state-linked groups;
- individual attackers.

Understanding threat actors is important because different actors may use different tools, techniques and objectives.

---

## 3.3 Indicator of Compromise (IOC)

An Indicator of Compromise (IOC) is a piece of technical information that may indicate malicious or suspicious activity.

Common examples include:

- IP addresses;
- domain names;
- URLs;
- file hashes;
- email addresses;
- malicious files.

For example, a SOC analyst may receive a suspicious IP address from a security alert and investigate it using threat intelligence sources.

---

## 3.4 Tactics, Techniques and Procedures (TTPs)

Tactics, Techniques and Procedures (TTPs) describe how an adversary operates.

**Tactics** describe the adversary's objective or reason for performing an action.

**Techniques** describe how an adversary achieves a tactical objective.

**Procedures** describe a specific implementation of a technique.

MITRE ATT&CK uses these concepts to organise and describe observed adversary behaviour.

---

## 3.5 Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)

Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) is intelligence collected from publicly available information.

Examples of OSINT sources include:

- public websites;
- security reports;
- public databases;
- news sources;
- search engines;
- publicly available technical information.

OSINT is important for CTI because analysts can combine information from multiple public sources to obtain additional context about a threat.

---

## 3.6 Security Operations Center (SOC)

A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a function or team responsible for monitoring, detecting, investigating and responding to cybersecurity events and incidents.

SOC analysts can use CTI to understand whether an alert may be related to a known threat, malware family, attack technique or threat actor.

---

## 3.7 Threat Intelligence Platform (TIP)

A Threat Intelligence Platform (TIP) is a system used to collect, organise, process, analyse and share threat intelligence.

One example that will be used later in our project is MISP.

---

## 3.8 Threat Hunting

Threat hunting is a proactive cybersecurity activity in which analysts search for suspicious or malicious activity that may not have been detected automatically.

Threat hunters can use CTI to create hypotheses and identify indicators or behaviours that should be investigated.

---

## 3.9 Security Alert

A security alert is a notification generated by a security system when potentially suspicious activity is detected.

Examples include:

- suspicious login;
- malware detection;
- unusual network traffic;
- suspicious PowerShell activity;
- communication with a suspicious domain.

---

## 3.10 False Positive

A false positive occurs when a security system identifies legitimate activity as potentially malicious.

False positives are important in SOC operations because a large number of unnecessary alerts can increase the workload of analysts.

CTI and additional context can help analysts investigate alerts more efficiently.

---

## 3.11 Large Language Model (LLM)

A Large Language Model (LLM) is an artificial intelligence model capable of processing and generating natural language.

In our project, an LLM is considered an analytical assistant for SOC analysts.

Potential uses include:

- summarising CTI reports;
- explaining technical information;
- organising information about indicators;
- assisting with investigation hypotheses;
- helping analysts process large amounts of text.

The LLM is not intended to replace the SOC analyst. The final interpretation and security decisions should remain under human control.

# 4. Classification of Cyber Threats

Cyber threats can be classified according to their objectives, methods and potential impact.

For this project, we selected several common categories.

| Threat Type | Description | Example Intelligence Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Phishing | Fraudulent messages or websites designed to deceive users and obtain information or access | Security reports, email security systems, CTI feeds |
| Malware | Malicious software designed to compromise, damage or disrupt systems | Malware databases, VirusTotal, CTI reports |
| Ransomware | Malicious software that can prevent access to data or systems and is commonly associated with extortion | ENISA reports, CTI reports |
| DDoS | Distributed denial-of-service activity intended to make services unavailable by overwhelming them with traffic | ENISA reports, network monitoring |
| Credential Theft | Theft of usernames, passwords or other authentication information | Authentication logs, CTI reports |
| Vulnerability Exploitation | Exploitation of weaknesses in software or systems | CVE databases, vendor advisories, CTI reports |
| Insider Threat | Harmful or risky activity performed by an authorised user, either intentionally or accidentally | Internal logs, SIEM, endpoint monitoring |
| Cyberespionage | Cyber activity focused on obtaining information or intelligence from a target | CTI reports, threat research |

# 5. Sources of Cyber Threat Intelligence

Threat intelligence can come from different types of sources. Combining several sources can provide more context than relying on a single source.

## 5.1 Open Sources

Open sources provide publicly available information.

Examples include:

- ENISA reports;
- public security research;
- public vulnerability databases;
- security blogs;
- public threat intelligence platforms;
- technical documentation.

Open sources are particularly useful for our academic project because they can be accessed and analysed without requiring private organisational data.

---

## 5.2 Internal Sources

Internal sources are generated within an organisation.

Examples include:

- firewall logs;
- SIEM events;
- authentication logs;
- endpoint detection alerts;
- network monitoring data;
- incident reports.

Internal data can provide information about what is happening inside a specific organisation.

---

## 5.3 Commercial Sources

Commercial threat intelligence providers can provide additional intelligence feeds and services.

These sources may contain information that is not publicly available.

For a real SOC, commercial intelligence can complement open-source and internal information.

---

## 5.4 Community Sources

Cybersecurity communities can share information about:

- malware;
- vulnerabilities;
- indicators of compromise;
- attack techniques;
- threat actors;
- security incidents.

Community information can be useful, but analysts should evaluate the reliability and context of information before using it.

---

# 6. MITRE ATT&CK

MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledge base that describes adversary behaviour based on real-world observations.

It organises adversary behaviour using concepts such as tactics, techniques, sub-techniques and procedures.

For example:

- **Tactic** — why an adversary performs an action.
- **Technique** — how an adversary achieves a tactical objective.
- **Sub-technique** — a more specific version of a technique.
- **Procedure** — a specific implementation of a technique observed in practice.

This framework is relevant to our project because future stages can use MITRE ATT&CK to connect CTI information with known attacker behaviour.

---

# 7. ENISA Threat Landscape

For Week 1, we reviewed the ENISA Threat Landscape 2026.

The ENISA Threat Landscape is an annual report that analyses the cybersecurity threat environment. The 2026 edition analyses incidents and events observed between 1 January and 31 December 2025.

According to ENISA, the current threat landscape continues to include recurring threats such as ransomware, DDoS activity, cybercrime, cyberespionage and hacktivist activity.

ENISA also highlights the growing role of artificial intelligence in cybersecurity. Emerging AI models are expected to be increasingly used to support malicious operations, while AI systems themselves can become part of the attack surface.

These observations are relevant to our project because our research focuses on the relationship between AI and SOC operations.

---

# 8. Main Findings from the ENISA Threat Landscape

The ENISA Threat Landscape 2026 highlights several important observations:

### 8.1 Ransomware

Ransomware remains one of the most impactful types of cyber incidents.

This demonstrates why SOC teams need effective threat intelligence and incident detection capabilities.

### 8.2 DDoS Activity

ENISA reports significant DDoS activity, including activity associated with hacktivist campaigns.

This shows that threat intelligence needs to include information about both technical attacks and the broader context in which attacks occur.

### 8.3 Cybercrime and Cyberespionage

The threat landscape continues to include cybercrime and cyberespionage.

These activities can involve different objectives, techniques and threat actors.

### 8.4 Artificial Intelligence

AI has a dual role in cybersecurity.

It can be used by defenders to support analysis and automation, but it can also be used by malicious actors to support cyber operations.

This is directly connected to our project because we are studying how LLMs can be used as an additional analytical capability for SOC analysts.
